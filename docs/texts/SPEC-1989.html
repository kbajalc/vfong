Algorithmic sequential decision-making in the frequency domain for life threatening ventricular arrhythmias and imitative artefacts: a diagnostic system

S. Barro, R. Ruiz, D. Cabello and J. Mira

Departamento de Electrónica, Facultad de Física, Universidad de Santiago de Compostela, Santiago de Compostela, Spain

Received March 1987, accepted November 1988

ABSTRACT

A preliminary study to approach the problem of reliably detecting life threatening ventricular arrhythmias in real time is described. An algorithm (DIAGNOSIS) has been developed in order to classify ECG signal records on the basis of the computation of four simple parameters calculated from a representation in the frequency domain. This algorithm uses a set of rules constituting an operative classification scheme based on the comparison of the parameters with a set of pre-established thresholds. This allows us to differentiate four general categories: ventricular fibrillation-flutter, ventricular rhythms, imitative artefacts and predominant sinus rhythm.

Keywords: ECG monitoring, ventricular arrhythmias, spectral analysis, clustering

INTRODUCTION

One of the most important tasks which can be implemented by an automatic monitor of cardiac arrhythmias within a coronary care unit (CCU) is the reliable detection and diagnosis in real time of those arrhythmias which represent a serious threat to the patient recovering from acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Among these, ventricular arrhythmias occupy a primary place, and in particular ventricular fibrillation, flutter and tachycardia, because of the haemodynamic deterioration which they produce. The reliable detection of these arrhythmias constitutes a challenge, not only in the monitoring of patients in the CCU, but also in other fields such as the design of automatic implantable defibrillators in which the defibrillatory electric shock is automatically initiated by the detection of these episodes 1,2,3 . Consequently, interest has arisen in the development of algorithms which could be transferred easily to a microprocessor system.

Unfortunately, the on-line electrocardiographic diagnosis of life-threatening arrhythmias is complex due to the fact that the records containing them show characteristics completely different from those of the rhythms for whose detection most arrhythmia monitors are designed. In normal records, the signal is processed on a beat-to-beat basis and all computation arises from the detection and identification of QRS complexes. Ventricular fibrillation or flutter, however, reflects the random and uncoordinated activity of myocardial muscle; the complexes as such disappear and a succession of waves with variable amplitude appear in the electrocardiogram. As a

result, these arrhythmias require a different type of analysis of the electrocardiographic signal, an analysis which is normally integrated in blocks of parallel processing so that only specific configurations of the signal induce its execution 4 .

On the other hand, the haemodynamic effects produced by ventricular flutter or fibrillation are sufficiently evident as to make one think that the pressure signals in the heart chambers might be much more indicative of the occurrence of these events 3 . The invasive nature of these techniques prevents their use as a routine procedure in all those cases where the follow-up and monitoring of a patient's cardiac rhythm is required. Consequently, we shall focus our attention on those techniques whose objective is the detection of arrhythmias by means of an analysis of electrocardiographic surface records.

The problem of automatically diagnosing these life threatening arrhythmias has been approached in different ways, utilizing techniques in both the time and frequency domains. Ventricular fibrillation (VF) is characterized in the frequency domain by the presence of a sinusoidal pattern with a narrow peak in the low frequency zone, which contrasts with the normal electrocardiographic records which have a much wider frequency distribution 4,5 . Imitative artefacts present characteristics in the frequency domain, which clearly distinguish them from real ventricular fibrillation 6 , and suggest the preferential use of frequency domain techniques to identify these arrhythmias reliably. In spite of this, because frequency analysis is usually costly in computing time, some authors suggest alternative methods of analysis in the time domain 7-10 .

The studies of Nygards and Hulting 5 , Herbschleb et al. 11 , Nolle et al. 6 , Foster and Weaver 12 and Murray

et al. 13 fit within a group in which the analysis is carried out in the frequency domain. The first of these establishes a recognition of the episodes of ventricular fibrillation and tachycardia by calculating the power spectra of signal segments suspected of having ventricular tachyarrhythmia. It determines the relative power contained in a frequency band around the maximum peak calculated from the frequency band 1.7–9 Hz. Depending on this relative power and on the frequency components of the maximum, by means of simple rules a discrimination is made between ventricular fibrillation and tachycardia. In the study of Herbschleb et al. 11 , the evolution of the power spectrum is analysed before and during the establishment of episodes of ventricular fibrillation which show a spectral organization which distinguishes them from noise and imitative artefacts. Nolle et al. 6 establish a comparison between the power spectra of episodes of ventricular fibrillation and imitative artefacts, analysing the power close to the peaks and their position. For their study, Foster and Weaver 12 divide the spectrum into three bands or domains, discriminating ventricular fibrillation and noise by computing the relative power contained in each domain. Finally, in the study of Murray et al. 13 , the power spectrum is analysed throughout episodes of ventricular fibrillation in order to assess the evolution of the position and shape of the peaks.

In this paper, we outline an approach to the problem of reliably detecting life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, in the frequency domain, by extracting a group of spectral parameters. This allows us to differentiate four general categories: ventricular fibrillation-flutter, paroxysmal tachycardia and ventricular rhythms, imitative artefacts and predominant sinus rhythm.

The increased computing time required for an analysis in the frequency domain is particularly inconvenient because continuous electrocardiographic monitoring must be accomplished on-line. The calculation tools used in our study are directed towards the removal of this handicap, with a view to its future incorporation into a prototype arrhythmia monitor currently in operation 14 ; we have made use of an FFT algorithm implemented in 32-bit Macroassembler to calculate the Fourier discrete transform coefficients of sequences having 1024 points; it is not expensive of computing time 15 .

ANALYSIS OF RECORDS

ECG records

The selection of a representative set of records is an important question concerned with both the choice of features to represent the records and the classification criteria to apply to those features. In our case the representation of records containing ventricular flutter or fibrillation is particularly important. Studies carried out in dogs 16 show that the spectra of the VF signals depend on the type of VF (spontaneous or induced, ischaemia or non ischaemic, etc.), indicating the intrinsic variability of such

episodes. Furthermore, these spectra vary throughout the development of such episodes, the spectrum of the signal showing a narrow peak and high organization at the beginning of the VF, and then the frequency components of the peak decreasing and disorganization increasing, as a function of the type of VF under analysis. The study of the ECG signal associated with VF processes in humans shows similar characteristics 13 . To the temporal variability of the spectrum in the course of each VF episode, one must add the considerable variability between patients.

However, the fatal nature of the episodes makes it necessary to act immediately VF signals are detected. This makes it possible to study their evolution in humans admitted to CCUs. Furthermore, as one of the main aims of the CCUs is the prophylaxis of this type of catastrophe, any premonitory arrhythmia is treated at once to prevent VF from occurring. Therefore the occurrence of VF, and, even more, its maintenance, are infrequent in a CCU. This considerably limits the set of records with very fast ventricular arrhythmic episodes and particularly during flutter and ventricular fibrillation.

We utilized a training set consisting of 55 ECG records selected from patients admitted to a CCU; they were classified according to the following four broad categories:

  1. 1. 13 records associated with episodes of ventricular flutter or fibrillation;
  2. 2. 15 records containing rhythms with complexes of aberrant morphology (paroxysmal tachycardias and ventricular rhythms);
  3. 3. 15 records of artifacts imitating the two previous categories;
  4. 4. 12 records containing predominant sinus rhythm.

The records were replayed into a digital computer using a sampling rate of 200 Hz. The sampled signals were segmented in sequences of 1024 points whose start was identified manually using a computer graphics terminal.

Even with the limitation expressed at the beginning of this section, the set of selected records belonging to the first of the aforementioned groups which we include in this study, extend to different types of VF episodes. Some with a high amplitude and high degree of spectral organization, which make the waves nearly sinusoidal are included ( Figure 1B ), together with others of lesser amplitude and a lower degree of spectral organization ( Figure 1A ).

The types of analysis proposed in this study is intended as a block of processing which should act in parallel with a primary processor performing a continuous analysis of the electrocardiographic record in the time domain 14,17 . This parallel processing must be triggered only when particular configurations of rhythm and morphology are presented, such as those described by Nygards and Hulting 5 . In the normal operation of the monitor within which this block will run, the predominant sinus rhythms, and a majority of episodes of tachycardia, will not trigger the frequency processing. We nevertheless consider them for the sake of completeness.

Preprocessing

The sequence of 1024 points were passed through a Hamming window to minimize the effects of segmentation on the spectra. A radix-2 FFT algorithm implemented in 32-bit Macroassembler 15 was applied to the sequences.

From the resulting complex coefficients, an estimation of the amplitude spectrum was made by approximating the modulus by the sum of the absolute values of the real and imaginary parts of the complex coefficients.

After calculating the amplitude spectrum, the maximum peak in the range of 0.5–9 Hz is searched. Afterwards, all components whose value is less than 5% of the value of this maximum are equated to zero. Thus, insignificant components away from the origin of the frequencies are not allowed to influence decisively the estimation of some of the descriptors subsequently produced.

Spectral features

From the representation in the frequency domain a set of concomitant characteristics emerges from among the records associated with a given category; it also discriminates among records associated with the other categories. We can summarize these spectral characteristics:

  1. 1. Ventricular flutter or fibrillation. These present a narrow and raised peak at the fundamental frequency with a spectrum relatively highly organized around this peak, and generally with small peaks at the immediate harmonics; there are no important components beyond the second harmonic ( Figure 1A and B ).

the calculated spectral descriptors are restricted to the range of the first 20 harmonics of the reference frequency, except in cases where this range is extended beyond 100 Hz, in which case it is this last frequency which is maintained as the interval higher limit. In spectral terms, the first 20 harmonics contain all the significant information. The range 0–0.5 Hz is rejected for the analysis in order to prevent components associated with baseline drifts from changing the values of the descriptors.

Specifically, the following features were obtained for each of the ECG segments analysed:

  1. 1. First spectral moment normalized (FSMN) or centre of gravity of the amplitude spectrum, expressed in multiples of the reference frequency and calculated in the range of the first 20 harmonics. The expression used for its calculation is:

\text{FSMN} = \frac{1}{F} \frac{\sum x_i f_i}{\sum x_i} \quad (1)

where F represents the reference frequency, x_i the value of the i th amplitude of the spectrum, f_i the frequency associated with this component and the sums are spread to the range earlier expressed. This descriptor represents a characterization of the way in which the amplitude spectrum is distributed around the reference frequency. Values next to the unit of the FSMN will indicate a very high concentration of area for the spectrum around the reference frequency, as in the case of records of VF. Larger values are associated with a larger dispersion of the area towards higher harmonics.

  1. 2. Ratio of the area contained within the band delimited by the origin of the frequencies, and half the reference frequency, as contrasted with the total area of the first 20 harmonics. We refer to this parameter as A1. It is a descriptor crucial in discriminating between fibrillations and imitative artefacts, given the regularity presented by the spectra of the former. In general, high values of this parameter are indicative of artefacts.
  2. 3. Ratio of the area contained in the range 0.7F to 1.4F ( F being the reference frequency), and the total area considered. We represent this parameter as A2 and it provides information about the relative distribution of area around the reference frequency. It is high in the VF category.
  3. 4. Ratio of the area contained around the second to the eighth harmonic, and the total area considered. To estimate this parameter, which we shall represent as A3, for each harmonic we compute the area contained in a band of 0.6F in width, centered on the harmonic. This descriptor is related to the repetition of peaks at frequencies which are multiples of the reference frequency. The parameter's value is high in the case of records with dominant sinus rhythm and is very low in the case of VF and imitative artefacts.

The spectral differences summarized in the preceding section are reflected in different values of these

descriptors. In the four-dimensional feature space clusters emerge which are produced by each of the categories ( Figure 2A–F ). They represent the projections of the training set on all the bidimensional sub-spaces of the feature space and clearly indicate the descriptors' appropriate choice, given the separation they produce between clusters corresponding to different categories.

DIAGNOSTIC ALGORITHM

It becomes evident that it is possible to carry out a statistical study with the object of demonstrating the existence of a significant cluster structure in the defined features space, each cluster representing a kind of rhythm or artefact. This study has been described elsewhere 18 . Nevertheless, independently of that study, and considering the distributions in the projections presented in Figure 2 , it is possible to deduce a set of simple rules to construct an operational classification scheme based on the comparison of the parameters to a set of thresholds.

Figure 3 presents the schematic of the DIAGNOSIS algorithm, which involves a simple version of the classifier, making use of visual information provided by the graphs of Figure 2 . As an important characteristic facing its practical implementation, it should be noted that it is not necessary to calculate all of the descriptors to determine the category associated with a record. Only for records associated with VF and some rhythms with complexes of aberrant morphology will it be necessary to know the value of all four descriptors.

As can be seen from Figure 3 , DIAGNOSIS presents four possible outputs associated with the categories of ventricular fibrillation-flutter (VF), series of complexes of aberrant morphology (CAM), artefacts (A), and a last category (?) associated with a lack of information needed to determine whether a record belongs to one of the previous categories. This again triggers the algorithm on a record at a later time. The retriggering process may be interrupted by the beat-to-beat processing or else by the same parallel processing when it generates an output different from '?'. The category associated with a predominant sinus rhythm has been considered only for the sake of completeness with regard to the analysis of the cluster to which it gives rise within the four-dimensional space; it does not have a specific output associated within the algorithm, since it will never trigger the parallel processing. Nevertheless, by analysing the values obtained for the descriptors on records of this type, the output produced by DIAGNOSIS for these records would be '?', such that the parallel processing will be halted at some point by the beat-to-beat processing without having generated an erroneous diagnosis.

sensitivity or correct detection ratio (CDR) is defined in % as:

\text{Sensitivity} = \text{CDR} \times 100 = \frac{TE - FN}{TE} \times 100 \quad (2)

where TE and FN represent the total number of events and the false negative, respectively, in each category. On the other hand, specificity is defined as the unit minus the false positive ratio ( FPR ), or:

\text{Specificity} = (1 - FPR) \times 100 = \left(1 - \frac{FP}{TE}\right) \times 100 \quad (3)

where FP represents the number of false positives.

In the calculation of these two parameters the two events in which the output of DIAGNOSIS was '?' have not been included, because an analysis of the records immediately afterwards resulted in their correct classification.

As is reflected in Table 2, we have given special importance to the sensitivity of the algorithm with respect to the category associated with VF because of the extreme gravity inherent in the VF processes, which necessitates a detection with a lesser number of possible false negatives.

CONCLUSIONS

We have proposed in this paper a scheme for the reliable detection of very fast arrhythmias of ventricular origin in patients recovering from AMI. This scheme uses a signal (the single-lead electrocardiogram) which does not involve invasive recording methods, because it is recorded as a matter of routine from all patients monitored in the CCUs. The proposed method represents a way of arriving at a diagnosis of arrhythmias, preventing the majority of the artefacts imitating them from being considered as arrhythmic episodes, and then activating alarms associated with them.

Given that our technique is intended as a block which must be added to a primary signal processor, the computation time spent on this second block of analysis is not critical as it was in the primary one, so that it is possible to increase the sophistication of the analysis to be used. Only fortuitously will there appear segments suitable for this analysis, which makes it possible to think (as in our case) of spectral descriptors as the starting point in the analysis.

The characteristic regularity of the spectra of the ECG records that correspond to real arrhythmia episodes have allowed us to justify a specific set of spectral features which discriminate between record groups. Observations on the clusters arising from records belonging to each class as they appear in the feature space, allow us to design a classification algorithm based on simple rules and which is therefore easily implementable within the arrhythmia monitor. The performance of such a classifier is good, since classification errors resulting from the detection of life-threatening arrhythmias are endowed with maximum priority.

To summarize, our system satisfactorily fulfils the requirement for which it has been designed: to prevent the great majority of artefacts imitating fast

ventricular arrhythmias from being considered as arrhythmic episodes. This avoids an excessive number of false positives in these episodes, which might encourage the clinical staff to become too accustomed to false alarms. This must not prevent the algorithm from having an implicit priority for the detection of every one of the real arrhythmic episodes; if it were to fail to detect only one of them, the consequences for the patient could be fatal.

Finally, the work of our group is dedicated to revealing and characterizing in formal terms the structure of clusters which give rise to spectral descriptors similar to those described in this paper. We can then develop a broad classification scheme in which the category boundaries arise as a consequence of the application of both main component selection methods, as well as formal techniques for revealing clusters in the new feature spaces.
